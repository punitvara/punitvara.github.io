📢 Ad Ranking in a Feed

Live demo
Interview guide

40,000 synthetic ad impressions over 14 days, 60 ads, with a hidden true click probability for every user and ad. Models train on days 0-9, calibrate on days 10-11 and are tested on days 12-13. Because the truth is known, every chart can show what the model believed next to what was real.

1. Click models compared

Four pCTR models on the test days

What to notice. Normalized entropy (NE) is log loss divided by the log loss of always predicting the average CTR, so below 1 means better than no model. All four models are close on AUC yet every one over-predicts by 25% or more (calibration above 1). An auction multiplies bid by pCTR, so a model that ranks well but is 25% high overcharges advertisers. That is why ads teams report NE and calibration, not AUC alone.

Logged CTR by feed position

What to notice. The ads shown in slot 5 would click as well as slot-1 ads if they were in slot 1 (see the "true CTR if slot 1" column), but their logged CTR is less than half because fewer people look that far down. Logged clicks mix ad quality with slot visibility. Section 6 shows what goes wrong if you train on that directly.

2. Feature hashing: memory against accuracy

Sparse IDs and crosses such as "interest x ad" are hashed into 2^bits weight slots.

What to notice. With 64 buckets, 94% of features share a weight with another feature and NE is poor. Quality saturates once buckets exceed the number of distinct features. In production the vocabulary is billions of IDs, so you pick bits by the NE-versus-memory curve, and collisions mostly hurt rare features.

3. Calibration

Sort test impressions by predicted CTR into 10 equal groups and compare the average prediction with the actual click rate in each.

x: predicted CTR, y: actual CTR. Points on the grey diagonal are perfectly calibrated.
What to notice. The calibration layer is a monotone map, so the ordering of ads and therefore AUC barely change. Log loss, NE and the predicted-to-actual ratio improve. It is fit on the most recent days so it also absorbs drift between training and serving.

4. Auction simulator

One feed request, five candidate ads. Edit bids, change the ranking rule, and add user-experience terms to the score.

Same request under each ranking rule

What to notice. The price per click is the smallest bid that would have kept the slot (generalized second price), so it is below the bid and depends on the next ad's score. Raise one ad's bid and the price paid by the ad above it rises. Ads marked * are clickbait: high click rate, high hide rate. Raise the hide penalty and they drop out, and with a large penalty a slot is left empty, which is how ad load becomes an output of ranking.

5. Revenue against user experience

1,500 simulated auctions with 8 eligible ads each. Sweep the hide penalty and watch revenue and "hide ad" reports.

Ranking objective comparison

pCTR-only ranking ignores bids, so no competitive price exists and the simulator charges the full bid. Compare it on advertiser value, CTR and hides, not revenue.
What to notice. A small hide penalty removes about a quarter of hides for a few percent of revenue. That short-term cost buys long-term feed usage, which no single auction can see. Bid-only ranking has the lowest CTR and advertiser value: it shows expensive ads nobody clicks. bid x pCTR gives the highest advertiser value, which is the argument for it.

6. Budget pacing

One advertiser, one day of 3,000 auctions, a fixed daily budget.

What to notice. Without pacing the budget is gone before midday and the advertiser misses cheaper evening traffic. Throttling (skip some auctions) spreads spend evenly. Lowering the bid instead wins only the cheapest impressions, so it buys the most clicks per dollar. Pacing is a control loop on top of the ranker, not part of the click model.

7. Pitfalls interviewers ask about

Position bias

What to notice. The old system put high bidders in top slots. A model trained without position learns "low bidders have low CTR" when the real cause was the slot. Predicted-to-true for low bidders is 0.42, so they almost never win slot 1 and never get the data to prove themselves. Training with position as a feature and scoring every candidate as if in slot 1 recovers most of it and learns the visibility curve on the right.

Miscalibration and the winner's curse

What to notice. Even a calibrated model over-predicts for the ads that win (1.14 here), because the auction picks the highest estimate and high estimates are partly luck. Add per-ad noise and winners are over-predicted by 2x. A global calibration layer fixes the average but not the per-ad error, so winners stay over-predicted. This is why new ads with few impressions need wider uncertainty or separate handling.

Negative downsampling without correction

x: predicted CTR, y: actual CTR.
What to notice. Dropping 90% of non-clicks makes training cheap, but the model then believes CTR is about 40% when it is 7%. Ranking (AUC) is untouched, which is why the bug is easy to miss. The fix is one line at serving: q = p / (p + (1 - p) / w), where w is the keep rate.

Delayed conversion labels

What to notice. A purchase can come days after the click. Training today on "no conversion yet" as a negative under-predicts conversion rate, worst for slow-converting advertisers, whose bids then come out too low. Waiting for labels fixes the bias but trains on stale data. Reweighting by the chance a conversion has arrived by now keeps the data fresh and removes part of the bias.
